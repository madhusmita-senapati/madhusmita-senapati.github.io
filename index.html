One product, five lenses

Teen Pay is a parent-linked UPI payment app for teens aged 13 to 19. Teens pay independently by QR code. Parents see every payment, set limits and follow learning progress. Short gamified lessons turn everyday spending into money skills. I worked on it with Team 2 across all five modules of the ICPM program.

Find the riskiest assumption before building anything

The problem

Indian teens want to learn about digital payments but lack confidence with modern money tools, and parents lack a structured, secure way to teach and monitor. Published research frames the gap: 96% of surveyed parents said their children lack money-management knowledge, 93% of teens wanted to learn about digital payments, and only 22% felt confident using them (muvin-Momspresso survey, 2022, reported by Mint; 600 teens and 600 parents). RBI's financial education strategy for 2020 to 2025 also treats financial literacy as a core life skill.

What we did
  • Built two personas, Arjun (16, wants independence) and Rajesh (45, wants his son to learn the value of money), with an empathy map for each side.
  • Mapped value proposition canvases for both users. They led to three product pillars: a Freedom payment suite, a gamified Wealth-Lab, and an automated Smart-Saver utility.
  • Listed five assumptions on incentives, parental trust, spending behaviour, market access and retention, then picked the one that matters most.
Key decision

The critical assumption: teens will engage with financial lessons inside a payment app if the lessons are gamified and tied to an immediate reward. If this is false, Teen Pay becomes one more wallet next to Google Pay and Amazon Pay. So the first experiment tests learning, not payments.

The experiment Planned

A Learn-to-Earn smoke test: social ads lead to a landing page with a 30-second explainer, a sample quiz and a waitlist for teens and parents. It is validated if more than 15% join the waitlist and more than 60% attempt the quiz. It is invalidated if users skip the learning content or acquisition cost is too high.

Design for a teen who wants freedom and a parent who wants visibility

The problem

The two users want opposite things. Teens want to pay without asking. Parents want to see and limit spending. A design that leans too far either way feels like surveillance to one and like risk to the other.

What we did
  • Wrote a user-centred design canvas covering users, context of use, pain points, key tasks, constraints and five design principles, led by freedom with guardrails.
  • Drafted a user research guide with interview scripts for teens and parents and a usability test plan.Planned
  • Wrote a PRD with 14 requirements across three releases, each traced to a user story, plus goals, non-goals, metrics and risks.
  • Wrote a DRD with the information architecture for the teen app and the parent app, an 11-screen inventory with success, failed and blocked states, and accessibility and microcopy rules.
  • Prototyped the core flows in Figma: onboarding and parent linking, Scan and Pay, Need vs Want tagging, limits and savings goals.

Key decision

Every blocked payment has to explain itself. When a spending limit stops a teen's payment, the teen sees why and the parent is alerted at the same moment. Both sides see the same facts, which keeps the limit from feeling arbitrary.

Turn a product vision into a backlog a team can build

The problem

A vision of safe financial independence for teens is not buildable until it is split into small, testable pieces and put in order. The team also had to show where Teen Pay sits against FamPay and FamApp, OmniCard, PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm and bank apps.

What we did
  • Wrote a vision statement and separate problem statements for the parent and the teen.
  • Grouped tasks with an affinity map, drew the interaction flow, and built story maps and journeys for both users.
  • Wrote epics and six user stories with acceptance criteria and story points on a Jira scrum board, from onboarding and parent linking to Scan and Pay, spending limits, Need vs Want, savings goals and payment failure handling.
  • Sorted the backlog into four tiers and ran a sprint board with To Do, In Progress and Done columns.

Key decision

Payments and parent visibility come first: profile and parent linking, secure UPI and QR payment, real-time notifications, history and support. Limits and Need vs Want follow, then quizzes, rewards, goals and summaries. The stock market simulator, multi-kid accounts and AI-generated stories wait.

Where Teen Pay differs

Competitors cover payments, parental controls or learning in pieces. Teen Pay is planned as the one product that combines controlled spending with financial learning.

Win the parent and the teen at the same time

The problem

The market is large: about 253 million adolescents in India, payments growing at 18.5% a year and 23.2 billion UPI transactions a month. Payments are also becoming a commodity, so payments alone cannot be the moat. The hard part of launch is that both a parent and a teen must onboard. 78% of parents interviewed wanted better visibility into teen spending.

What we did
  • Chose the first segment: teens 13 to 19 and parents 38 to 50 in middle and high income urban households, starting in six metros and expanding to Ahmedabad, Jaipur, Chandigarh and Indore.
  • Wrote a positioning statement and separate messages: "Spend independently. Learn responsibly." for teens, and financial freedom with visibility for parents.
  • Set a three-tier pricing model: Free at ₹0, Smart at ₹999 a year, and Family+ at ₹1,499 a year for up to three teens.
  • Planned a ₹15 lakh quarter that ramps from test to optimise to scale: ₹7.5 lakh for social and paid media, ₹4.5 lakh for partnerships and SEO, and ₹3 lakh for content.

Module 5 · Measure

Data-Driven Product Management

  • Problem validation plan
  • Product-market fit framework
  • Metric definitions
  • Growth roadmap

Decide in advance what counts as proof

The problem

Waitlist sign-ups are easy to collect and easy to misread. A product for families only works if they link accounts, keep paying and keep learning after the launch rewards end.

What we did
  • Planned problem validation interviews with teens and parents before testing any solution.
  • Defined smoke test measures with exact formulas: waitlist conversion as unique sign-ups divided by unique visitors, and quiz completion as completers divided by starters, each reported with sample size and traffic source.
  • Defined three pilot measures: family activation (linked and first payment within 7 days), week-four payment retention (a payment on days 22 to 28), and repeat learning (another lesson within 7 days of the first).
  • Added business measures for premium conversion, customer acquisition cost, lifetime value with stated assumptions, separate parent and teen NPS, and support quality, plus channel CAC and referral yield.
Key decision

Gate each stage on evidence. The roadmap moves from validating demand, to proving family value in a live pilot, to scaling sustainably, and each step needs its own decision evidence. Waitlist interest counts as an early signal. Sustained use and learning outcomes count as stronger proof.
